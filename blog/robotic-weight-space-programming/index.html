Repairing Robot Policies with Weight Space Programming

Frontier Models can restore task success for vision-based behavior cloning policies under distribution shifts, directly in weight space

Jannick Stranghöner1* Philipp Hartmann1* Felix Störck1*

Barbara Hammer1 Klaus Neumann1,2

1CITEC, Bielefeld University 2Fraunhofer IOSB-INA *Equal contribution

TL;DR

  • Recent work (“Sparks of Weight-Space Programming” by Takehi et al. [1]) has explored language models that natively read and write the weights of other neural networks. We bring this idea to robot learning and find that LLMs are surprisingly good at reasoning about why closed-loop, vision-based policies fail.
  • We train a robot arm to pick up a cube and put it in a tray from camera images, by imitating demonstrations, and then break it with small distribution shifts.
  • To diagnose the failures, the LLMs find the neurons the trained policy never uses and probe the network with tools from mechanistic interpretability.
  • To fix them, they write small programs into those unused neurons: controllers that switch on only when needed, such as lowering the cube once the wrist camera sees the tray below.
  • In doing so, the LLMs give the policy qualitatively new capabilities. When the tray's location is no longer static, three of the LLMs wire a readout from the cameras into the network that tells the arm where to carry the cube: spatial generalization that was not present in the original policy.
  • Across five experiments, the best edits raise task success from 0–5% to between 33% and 95%. And the skill seems to grow with model capability: the newest model we tested (Fable 5.2) does best in all experiments.
  • All of this works without gradient-based optimization or fitting, and without a single new demonstration, using only a fixed budget of test runs.
  • Throughout the history of robot learning, a failing policy has meant more data: more demonstrations, more robot time, another round of training. This may be changing: robot policies could soon be debugged and repaired natively in weight space, by LLMs that reason about why the robot fails.

Teaching your robot new skills has never been easier: low-cost robots have boomed, open datasets keep growing, and robot foundation models are closer than ever to working out of the box.

So say you collected some demonstrations, fine-tuned your favourite VLA and deployed it on your 3D-printed robot of choice. Then, in certain states, it just does not work. It tries to grasp something, fails and loops. It stalls halfway through the task. Something as simple as a change in lighting throws it off. What do you do?

For most roboticists, the answer is more data (e.g. DAgger [2]) and another round of training. We skip both and instead present early results showing that a frontier LLM can identify the failure modes that appear in closed loop and iterate on fixes, entirely in weight space.

Behaviour Cloning and Its Limits

Learning robust behaviour by imitating humans is a long-standing goal in robotics. Behaviour cloning is attractive because it is simple: it turns a control problem into a supervised learning problem.

For the same reason, it is also hard: it is fundamentally limited by distribution shift. The policy learns from the state distribution of its demonstrator, but when rolled out, it visits its own. Each small mistake takes it into states the demonstrator never saw, where bigger mistakes become more likely. This is known as compounding error [3], and it happens even in the very environment the policy was trained in.1 It only gets worse under the obvious shifts, like a visual distractor or a new object.

Yet much of what a policy has learned often stays useful under a shift, which suggests that a fix may be small and surgical. But where would we make it? To us, a trained policy is a black box. The only lever we have is the data it learns from.

Opening the Black Box

LLMs already sit at the heart of robot learning. Vision-language models are the backbone of today's robot foundation models (OpenVLA [4], π0 [5]), and LLMs write robot code (Code as Policies [6]) or even control robots directly [7]. But none of them can look inside a trained policy and tell us why it fails.

That is starting to change. In Sparks of Weight-Space Programming [1], LLMs read and write a network's weights directly, without an optimizer, which the authors call weight space programming. In one of many striking demonstrations, GPT-6 Astra is handed a policy whose car gets stuck on a ramp in every episode. From the raw weights and one recorded episode, it explains why, then rewrites 3.5% of the weights in a single reply. Now the car succeeds every time. LLMs, it seems, can reason in weight space about how a policy behaves. While recent work applies this to specific aspects of a robot foundation model, such as making π0.5 move faster [8] or follow paraphrased instructions more reliably [9], the rest of this blog is dedicated to studying whether LLMs can rescue policies that no longer work at all, by finding out in closed loop why they fail and fixing them in weight space.

The Setup

To study this in a controllable and reproducible way, we test it on a standard robotic manipulation task in simulation: cube pick-and-place. Our suite builds on OCBench [10], with one slight tweak. A tray now marks where the cube goes, which gives us a goal we can move later to introduce distribution shift.

To train a policy, we need demonstrations. Here we script a near-optimal expert that collects them for us, using privileged information such as the true position of the cube. This expert is memoryless and only reacts to the current state of the world; it is Markov.

The policy is a neural network that sees three camera images and the robot's own state. A pretrained ResNet [11] compresses each image into 256 numbers, and a small network with three layers of 256 ReLU neurons maps everything to target angles for the joints and a command for the gripper. Like the expert, the policy only sees the present, so it is Markov as well, and in principle it can imitate the expert perfectly. The difference lies in what they see. The expert reads the simulator's true state, while the policy has to infer it from images.

If we simply train the policy to reproduce the expert's actions, it fails almost completely at test time. Ironically, the expert is too good; it never makes a mistake, so its data never shows how to recover from one, and the policy runs straight into the compounding error described above. The fix may seem counterintuitive: we run the expert with noise added to its actions, so its demonstrations also visit states slightly off its own path, and the recorded actions show how it steers back. The policy learns to recover from its own small mistakes, which keeps it in-distribution even in closed loop. With this recipe, known as DART [12], our policy succeeds in 95.8% of held-out test episodes after training on 8,426 demonstrations.2

This trained policy is where every experiment starts. We drop it into a situation it was never trained for, watch it fail, and ask an LLM to restore its performance. The LLM can act only by changing the 335,111 parameters of the MLP on top of the frozen ResNet, numbers that come with no labels and no explanation. To make that a fair and repeatable test, a harness controls everything else, from what the LLM gets to see to how its final edit is scored.

What it getsThe policy's weights in a Python kernel, 200 demonstrations from the training environment, and a recording of every test it has run. It can run the network on these recordings as often as it likes, but the kernel has no simulator.
How it testsTo see the robot move, it spends one of 25 test rounds. Each round runs its current edit on the same 100 practice episodes in the changed environment.
What is off-limitsGradients, optimizers, fitting and automated search. To write the strongest policy it can, it has to edit the weights itself.
How it is scoredIts final edit runs on 500 new episodes it never sees, from the same starts as the original policy, in some experiments with a variant it was never shown. A cube counts once it has been lifted and then reaches the tray.3

Five Ways to Break a Policy

To find out whether an LLM can repair a failing policy, we first need one that fails. We move the goal, once or in every episode. We recolour the cube, declare a zone off-limits, and swap the expert for one that behaves like a human. Every change collapses the original policy from 96% to near 0% task success. Then an LLM gets to work. Some frontier models even found a loophole in our success test and used it to score. In each experiment we present the best-performing LLM without such an exploit. The exploits are covered later in the case studies.

Experiment 5 of 5 · Non-Markovian Demonstrations

A change in the data. Technically, this is not a distribution shift; it changes the data the policy learns from. The demonstrations of experiments 1 to 4 came from a Markov expert, a feedback controller that reacts only to the current state. Humans rarely work that way. Our behaviour is correlated over time: we act on intentions nobody else can see, follow a rough plan, speed up and slow down, hesitate before we commit, and sometimes miss and try again.

OCBench comes with experts that mimic these properties, and making a script behave like this takes a lot of randomness. Ours plans each episode as a smooth curve through a few random waypoints, at a different speed on every stretch. It grasps the cube from a random direction with the wrist tilted by up to 25°, pauses with its fingers around the cube in about four out of ten episodes, and tries again when a grasp fails. We record it exactly as before and train the same network on it.

This creates an expressivity mismatch [10]. The data comes from a non-Markovian expert, while our policy is still Markov. For an intuition, consider the moment the expert pauses with its fingers around the cube. Throughout the pause, the policy sees the same state and has no notion of how long the pause has lasted, so it could simply learn to stay in that spot forever, a failure case analysed in detail in [13]. Just by training on this data, the closed-loop success of our policy drops from 95.8% to 0.4%.

Instead of making our policy non-Markovian, we ask: can an LLM make it succeed as often as the policy trained on the Markov expert, without a single new demonstration?

After the ExperimentsUnder the Hood

So far, we have judged the edits mostly from the outside, by how the robot behaves and how often it succeeds. This section is about how exactly those changes are implemented in weights. For three edits, we open the network and trace how the new behaviour is implemented in the weights: a stabilizing loop that helps the policy stay in-distribution, a goal detector that Opus builds on high-dimensional camera features, and a full-on program that keeps its memory in the robot's own pose, but with some caveats.

Case Study 1 · Opus · Experiment 5A Recovery the Expert Never Taught

In experiment 5, the policy learns from a non-Markovian expert that behaves more like a human. It follows a latent plan and never demonstrates how to recover from slight disturbances, causing the policy to drift out of distribution. Opus identifies where exactly this happens: whenever the gripper tilts slightly, the network turns the wrist further in the same direction, so the tilt grows by about 9% with every control step. In one rollout, the gripper reaches the cube tilted by about 30°, far beyond the roughly 7° of the demonstrations, and the fingers close next to the cube instead of around it.

Opus's fix is a simple proportional controller, written into units the network never uses, that measures the tilt and turns the wrist back. From a 10° tilted start, the original policy lets the tilt grow to about 25° within two seconds; with the circuit, it is down to 1° after half a second. Switching the circuit off drops the finished edit from 68 to 3 successes in 100 evaluation runs.

In the real world, robots mostly learn from non-Markovian data, because their experts are humans. Learning from such data usually means changing the policy itself, most prominently with action chunking [14], which recent work traces back to the temporal correlations in human demonstrations [10]. This preliminary result suggests that part of what a memoryless policy loses on such data might instead be recovered after training, directly in its weights.

Case Study 2 · Opus · Experiment 2A Goal Detector in 256 Dimensions

The second case study comes from experiment 2, where the goal moves every episode. Only the cameras can tell the policy where the goal is, through the embedding of a frozen ResNet. This embedding is sub-symbolic, i.e., the goal is spread across all 256 embedding dimensions, so there is no single neuron Opus could point to.

So Opus first tries to separate correlation from causation (to anthropomorphize a little). It takes frames in which the policy begins to descend, swaps in one camera's embedding from frames in which it hovers, and checks whether the descent survives. It finds out that swapping the wrist camera alone removes the descent, and the rollouts show that this trigger works anywhere on the table. All of this was evaluated on recorded rollouts without a simulator, so in principle it would work on a real robot too.

Opus then builds a small circuit on the wrist camera. The weights w of one idle unit are a difference of means, the average wrist embedding with the goal right beneath the gripper minus the average with the goal farther away. The unit fires only while the gripper holds the cube, and two copy-units turn it into a straight-down motion that Opus derived from the arm's kinematics.

Opus finds structure in a high-dimensional embedding without external optimization, confirms it causally, and builds on it. Across both case studies, it draws on three fields: activation patching [15] and difference-of-means probes [16] from mechanistic interpretability, a stable feedback loop from control theory, and a straight-down motion from the kinematics of the arm. Repairing a policy is an interdisciplinary problem, and Opus treats it as one.

Case Study 3 · Fable 5.1 · Experiment 2Memory in a Memoryless Network

Sadly, the most ingenious edit in our experiments is not a repair at all. In experiment 2, Fable 5.1 quickly concludes that the policy cannot see the goal, even though three other LLMs read it from the same camera features. Therefore, on its first pass, Fable writes a program of its own into more than a hundred idle units, and this program switches the trained network off as soon as the cube is lifted. It drags the cube across the table in ever wider sweeps until the cube happens to pass over the goal. Fable even checked the program in a small kinematic simulation it wrote itself before its first rollout. That rollout scored 86 of 100.

While this exploits an underspecified success condition, the more interesting part is how the behaviour is implemented in a memoryless policy. The program has to know whether the search is running and which row of the sweep comes next, but the policy only maps the current images and robot state to an action. Fable's solution is to keep the memory in the robot's body. Once the gripper holds a lifted cube, the network twists the wrist past an angle the original policy never reaches, and from then on it reads that twist back as a flag. An out-of-distribution state has become a one-bit memory.

As long as the flag is set, two units silence every unit the original uses in its last hidden layer, and the sweep takes over. The current row is stored the same way, in the shoulder angle. Each of the eight rows, from 30 to 64 cm out, has its own band of shoulder angles. The base swings one way on even rows and back on odd ones, and at the end of a row, the target moves on to the next. The shoulder follows into the next band, and that is how the counter goes up by one. The network itself still remembers nothing. Its memory is the robot's pose.

Our task prompt says that a cube dragged into the goal does not count, but our success check only required an earlier lift. Fable lifted the cube first and then dragged it, which earned it 93.0% on the unseen episodes. It was not the only one: three of the five LLMs exploited this gap in experiment 2, Fable 5.2 almost perfectly and Sonnet 5.5 much less successfully. Weight space programming, it turns out, is just as vulnerable to reward hacking [17] as any black-box optimizer. At least Fable was honest about it.

Since the container cannot be perceived, the edit exploits the success rule: a cube that has been lifted ≥3 cm and is then set down and dragged along the table counts as placed the moment it passes within 4 cm of the container centre.

So What?Implications for Robot Learning

For decades, robotics has tried to replace hand-written controllers with behaviour learned from data, from a neural network that steered a vehicle from camera images in 1989 (ALVINN [18]) to the first serious proposals of imitation learning as a route to capable robots [19]. In the last few years, the field has finally scaled this up, with foundation models trained on large and diverse robot data (OpenVLA [4], π0 [5]). Yet LLMs entered robotics the way engineers always had, by writing code for rewards (Eureka [20]) and for control (Code as Policies [6]). The weights of a learned policy remained, until recently, the one part of a robot's software that only an optimizer could write. We show early signs that language models can read and rewrite the learned controller itself.

This opens a shortcut in robot learning, as illustrated by the magenta path in the visual abstract. A language model can debug a trained policy the way a programmer debugs code. It opens the black box, finds out why the policy fails, and writes the fix into its weights. In our five experiments, this took policies from 0–5% to 33–95% task success, without a single new demonstration or gradient step. And because each fix is a small circuit with a stated purpose, it can be read, tested and switched off. That matters for policies that are notoriously hard to evaluate [21], and therefore hard to deploy.

If this capability keeps improving, it could open up possibilities that are new to robot learning, or at least far easier to reach than before.

  • Repair from rollouts. Throughout our experiments, the LLMs worked from recorded rollouts. They could ask for test runs, but could never query the simulator itself. A deployed robot might one day be repaired the same way, from the rollouts it records anyway.
  • Finding faults in the weights. Opus traced the drifting wrist to a positive feedback loop it could measure directly in the network. Faults like this might be found by inspecting a policy rather than by waiting for it to fail.
  • New behaviours on demand. In experiment 4, Opus made a trained policy stay out of a forbidden zone, without a single demonstration of how to avoid it. New goals and safety rules might one day be added the same way.
  • Learned and engineered in one network. In case study 1, Opus wrote a textbook controller into a learned policy. Perception could come from data, while critical control loops are written in, readable and testable.

Coding agents show where this could go. When GPT-3 turned out to write simple programs, its authors found this "exciting because GPT-3 was not explicitly trained for code generation" [22]. On their benchmark, it solved none of the problems. Then labs began to train for code, later with reinforcement learning that rewards code for passing compilers and unit tests (DeepSeek-R1 [23]), and coding agents took off. Weight space programming today looks much like code generation did then. It emerges incidentally from what a model knows about code, control theory and mechanistic interpretability, and nobody has trained for it yet. Robot learning, meanwhile, has long judged its policies by whether they succeed, checked automatically in simulation benchmarks (LIBERO [24]) or labelled by hand on real robots [21]. These success labels are much like the verifiable rewards that let LLM post-training flourish (Tülu 3 [25]), and they are what we used to score every edit in this blog. In principle, modern robot learning pipelines already have everything needed to train language models for weight space programming. Let's see where that takes us.

Not long ago, a trained robot policy was a black box, even to the people who built it. Handing it to a language model for repair would have sounded like science fiction. Soon, it might be the first thing we try when a robot fails.
